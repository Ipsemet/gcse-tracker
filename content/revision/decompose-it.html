Decompose It

Abstraction, decomposition and algorithmic thinking are three ideas that sound obvious and are marked on whether you can actually use them. So this page makes you use them: fill in the gallery, build a structure diagram that obeys the three rules, write the organiser out from memory, then sit the 21-mark paper.

The three sentences

Step 01 · 4 min

Learn these as sentences. Then never write only these — every mark above the definition comes from applying them to the problem in front of you.

Define abstraction.[2]
Removing or hiding detail that is not needed, so that only what matters for a particular purpose remains. Both halves score. Without the purpose, “unnecessary” means nothing — the Tube map is brilliant for choosing a train and useless for judging a walk.
Define decomposition.[1]
Breaking a large problem into smaller sub-problems, each of which can be solved on its own.
Two benefits of decomposing.[2]
Each part is easier to solve and test on its own; different people can work on different parts at once; a solved part can be reused. Two different benefits — “easier” twice is one mark.
Define algorithmic thinking.[1]
Working out the precise, ordered steps that solve the problem — and would solve it again with different inputs. The test: could someone follow it without making a judgement of their own?
What does a structure diagram show?[1]
How a problem breaks down into sub-problems. Not the order things happen in — that is a flowchart.
Abstraction or decomposition?—
Decomposition breaks a problem up. Abstraction strips detail out. One is about size, the other about detail.
